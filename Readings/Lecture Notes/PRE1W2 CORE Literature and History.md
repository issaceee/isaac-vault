## Introduction: The Problem of Context  

Professor **Joe Moshenska** begins with a thought experiment: imagine that one of your own writings — a diary, an essay, even a note — is discovered centuries in the future. Scholars of that future, desperate for traces of the early twenty-first century, pore over every sentence as evidence of its age.  

But what would count as your “context”? Your education, gender, language? The climate crisis? The internet age? Or things you yourself never noticed shaping your thought?  

> **Mini-Essay — The Mirage of Context**  
> “Context” feels like a bridge between text and history, but it often hides what it claims to reveal. To *put* a work “in context” assumes that the text is inert, waiting to be contained by its background. Moshenska’s thought experiment exposes the fiction of containment: context is not a tub for margarine, but a web of invisible forces — many of which the writer never perceives:contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}.

### The Double Meaning of “Context”  

The Oxford English Dictionary traces *context* to the Latin *contextus* — “that which is woven together.” Originally, it referred to **the relations within a text**, not its surroundings. Milton described the Bible as “that book within whose sacred context all wisdom is enfolded.”  

Over time, the word’s sense flipped outward: from the inner weave of a text to the external network around it. Literature, however, still operates in both senses — as a tissue of interrelations within itself and with history.  

> **Mini-Essay — The Text as Its Own Context**  
> Every work of art is both container and contained. The texture of its language, its repetitions, and silences form a history of their own — not commentary upon the world but sediment of it. To read contextually, then, is also to read **the history within form**.  

---

## I. Scales of Context  

Moshenska asks: how large or small can context be?  
- **Micro**: biographical details — age, gender, class.  
- **Meso**: institutions — the university, the city, the nation.  
- **Macro**: historical epochs — capitalism, empire, “the internet age.”  

A writer may be unaware of these frameworks, yet they structure her imagination. Contexts are always **multiple** and **partly unconscious**. The author is not necessarily “the best arbiter” of her own contexts.  

---

## II. Always Historicise! — Fredric Jameson  

Fredric Jameson’s dictum from *The Political Unconscious* (1981) provides a rallying cry: **“Always historicise!”**  

Yet, as Jameson argues, historicising can proceed along **two paths**:contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}:  
1. **The path of the object** — the historical origins of a text’s forms and contents.  
2. **The path of the subject** — the historical origins of our interpretive categories themselves.  

The second path radicalises the first: *our very ways of reading are historical.* Thought itself has a history.  

> **Mini-Essay — The Historicity of Understanding**  
> To read historically is not merely to reconstruct a vanished world but to recognise that our own minds belong to history. Interpretation is itself an event in time — part of the story literature tells about how humans make meaning.  

---

## III. From New Criticism to New Historicism  

### The Reaction Against Autonomy  

The **New Critics** (Cleanth Brooks, Wimsatt, Ransom) treated poems as self-contained “well-wrought urns.” New Historicists rejected this autonomy, insisting that texts are **sites of social energy** rather than sealed aesthetic objects.  

### Stephen Greenblatt and the “Circulation of Social Energy”  

In *Shakespearean Negotiations* (1988), Greenblatt reads literature as participating in the cultural flows of its age — political, religious, economic:contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2}.  

His essay “Invisible Bullets” begins not with Shakespeare but with a spy’s note reporting Marlowe’s heresies. From there, he links Elizabethan espionage, colonial exploration, and *Henry IV*’s politics.  

> “We are like Harriot, surveying a complex new world… and still more like the Indians, compelled to pay homage to a system of beliefs whose fraudulence only confirms their power.”  

This method — the **anecdotal opening** — became the trademark of New Historicism: beginning with a curious fragment of history and connecting it to a literary text through analogy rather than direct influence.  

> **Mini-Essay — The Anecdote as Method**  
> The anecdote embodies the New Historicist belief that history’s meaning is dispersed, not hierarchical. A court record, a travel diary, a play: all transmit the same “social energy.” The critic’s task is to trace these invisible currents rather than to prove causation.  

### Limits of the Method  

Moshenska notes that this levelling of text and context often re-established old hierarchies. Shakespeare remained the privileged mediator; the “circulation” still revolved around canonical centres. Moreover, treating entire epochs as coherent systems of energy risks reproducing the **periodisation** New Historicism sought to escape.

## IV. Beyond the Period: Rethinking Temporality  

Recent theory unsettles the very notion of linear, unified time.  

### Michel Serres — The Polychronic World  

> “Every historical era is multitemporal, simultaneously drawing from the obsolete, the contemporary, and the futuristic… an object is thus polychronic, multitemporal, and reveals a time that is gathered together, with multiple pleats.”:contentReference[oaicite:3]{index=3}  

Time, for Serres, is not a line but a **folded fabric**. Literature embodies this folding — carrying past and future within its present.  

### Bruno Latour — We Have Never Been Modern  

Latour extends Serres’s insight: “Modern temporality is the result of a retraining imposed on entities which would pertain to all sorts of times.” The illusion of a continuous, progressive flow depends on forcing everything contemporary into the same time. Once we see “quasi-objects” — hybrids of past and future — the “beautiful order” of history collapses into a “great hotchpotch.”  

> **Mini-Essay — The Pleated Present**  
> To read a text as “of its time” is already to repress the anachronisms it carries. A poem is never modern or medieval alone; it folds many moments into one. The act of reading unfolds those pleats, releasing the future hidden in the past.  

### Queer Temporalities  

**Elizabeth Freeman** and **Carolyn Dinshaw** push this temporal critique into queer theory. Freeman’s term **“chrononormativity”** names the discipline by which societies regulate individuals through time — marriage, reproduction, career, decline. Dinshaw’s *How Soon Is Now?* (2012) celebrates **“queer anachronism”**: the refusal to fit neatly into historical sequence.  

These approaches make temporality itself a political and aesthetic question. To “place” a text in time is never neutral; it is to normalise it.  

### Rita Felski — The Busy Afterlife of the Text  

> “The busy afterlife of the literary artefact refutes our efforts to box it into a moment of origin.”:contentReference[oaicite:4]{index=4}  

Texts act across time. They produce effects long after their creation — changing readers, shaping culture, generating new histories.  

> **Mini-Essay — The Text as Temporal Actor**  
> Literature does not merely belong to history; it *makes* history. Each reading reactivates the work’s potential energies. The poem survives not as residue of the past but as participant in the present — and architect of future meanings.  

---

## V. Case Study: John Donne’s *“The Relic”*  

To illustrate these theories, Moshenska turns to Donne’s *The Relic* (c.1611) — a poem obsessed with time, resurrection, and misunderstanding.  

> *“When my grave is broke up again, / Some second guest to entertain…”*  

The speaker imagines his bones exhumed, mistaken for saintly relics. Donne’s conceit fuses the **earthly and eternal**, the **sacred and profane**.  

Historically, relics were outlawed in Protestant England. Donne, a convert from Catholicism, knew their controversy intimately. A historicist might read the poem as commentary on post-Reformation piety — and rightly so. Yet, as Moshenska argues, *The Relic* also performs a deeper **temporal experiment**.  

Relics themselves are *objects out of time* — decayed matter imbued with divine life. Donne’s poem mimics this paradox: it survives as a relic of language, a body of words animated by new readers.  

> **Mini-Essay — The Poem as Relic**  
> *The Relic* enacts what it describes. It is unearthed by each generation, reinterpreted, mistaken, adored. Its temporality is “pleated”: future, present, and past intertwine. To read it is to take part in the miracle of its misrecognition.  

Donne’s later prose (*Devotions upon Emergent Occasions*) makes this temporal anxiety explicit:  

> “Before you sound that word *present*, or that monosyllable *now*, the present and the now is past.”:contentReference[oaicite:5]{index=5}  

For Donne, as for Serres, time itself is unstable — a vanishing instant. His poetry thus anticipates the theorists who see every moment as **multitemporal**.  

---

## VI. Conclusion: Literature as Multitemporal Thought  

To “historicise” is not to trap a work in its past but to perceive its **movement through time**. A literary text is a **polychronic artefact**: it contains the residues of its origins, the transformations of its readers, and the futures it continues to invent.  

> **Mini-Essay — Reading in Deep Time**  
> The act of reading is itself historical. When we study Donne, we are not returning to 1611 but creating a new intersection of moments — his, ours, and those to come. Literature and history are not separate disciplines but intertwined modes of thinking temporally.

**For Paper 1B (Introduction to Literature and History):**  
- Develops conceptual precision about *context*, *periodisation*, and *temporality*.
